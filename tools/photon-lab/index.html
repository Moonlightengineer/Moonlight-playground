Photon Lab

When can one photon knock out an electron, lift it to a higher level, or tear it away from the atom? Change the photon and watch what happens.

Three rules for one photon

Metal surface

Photoelectric emission

hf ≥ φ

One photon gives all its energy to one electron. It escapes only if the photon energy is at least the work function. KEmax = hf − φ.

Hydrogen atom

Jump to a higher level

hf = Eupper − Elower

The photon is absorbed only if its energy exactly matches the gap between two energy levels. Otherwise it passes straight through.

Hydrogen atom

Ionisation

hf ≥ 13.6 / n² eV

Any photon with at least the ionisation energy removes the electron completely. The extra energy becomes the free electron's kinetic energy.

Try this

  1. Choose Zinc, set λ = 500 nm and turn the intensity to 10. Do any electrons escape? Why not?

    Show answer

    No. Each photon has only about 2.49 eV, which is less than zinc's 4.3 eV. More photons per second does not help, because one electron cannot collect energy from two photons.

  2. Find the longest wavelength that still ejects electrons from sodium.

    Show answer

    λ₀ = hc/φ ≈ 1243 eV nm ÷ 2.3 eV ≈ 540 nm. At 541 nm and above, nothing comes out.

  3. With sodium at 400 nm, change the intensity. What happens to KEmax and to the number of electrons?

    Show answer

    KEmax stays the same, because it depends only on f and φ. The number of electrons per second goes up with intensity.

Emission spectrum

    Try this

    1. Starting from n = 1, fire an 11.00 eV photon. Is it absorbed?

      Show answer

      No. 11.00 eV is more than the 10.20 eV gap to n = 2 but does not match any gap exactly, and it is less than 13.6 eV. The photon cannot give away "just part" of its energy, so it passes through.

    2. Put the electron in n = 2. What is the smallest photon energy that ionises the atom?

      Show answer

      13.6 / 2² = 3.40 eV. Try 3.39 eV and 3.40 eV.

    3. Excite the electron to n = 4 (12.75 eV from ground) and tick "Show every possible downward transition". How many spectral lines are possible?

      Show answer

      6 lines: 4→3, 4→2, 4→1, 3→2, 3→1, 2→1. In general n(n − 1)/2.

    4. Which downward jumps give visible light?

      Show answer

      Jumps that end at n = 2 (Balmer series), e.g. 3→2 (red, about 656 nm) and 4→2 (blue-green). Jumps to n = 1 are ultraviolet; jumps to n = 3 are infrared.

    DSE-style practice

    Original questions written in DSE style (not past-paper questions). Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

      Notes for teachers

      • Constants follow the HKDSE data sheet, so hc ≈ 1243 eV nm. Calculated wavelengths can differ from measured values by about 0.3% (e.g. 658 nm instead of the measured 656 nm for 3→2).
      • Work functions are typical textbook values. Your textbook may quote slightly different numbers.
      • Hydrogen levels are shown up to n = 6. The simulator accepts a photon within ±0.02 eV of an energy gap, standing in for the small natural width of real spectral lines.
      • Photoelectrons are drawn leaving at KEmax speed. In reality many leave with less energy, because some energy is lost before they reach the surface.
      • Collisions with electrons (which can give part of their kinetic energy to the atom) are not simulated here.
